A facebook ads budget for a Shopify store is not a universal daily number. Meta tells you when an ad set has enough results to leave learning. Shopify’s own article mentions a one-dollar floor and a five-dollar suggestion. Independent shops publish playbooks that start at five, ten or twenty dollars. They are allowed to disagree. This is how to pick a minimum Meta ads budget you can afford to lose, and how the learning phase should change your patience, not your slogan.

:::takeaways
- Meta: an ad set usually exits learning after about 50 results in the week after the last significant edit. That is results, not a dollar minimum.
- Shopify’s 2025 cost article says the minimum budget for impression-billed ads is $1 a day, and recommends $5 a day for traffic or sales campaigns. Check Ads Manager for the minimum on your account.
- Talk Shop’s 2026 matrix starts new shops at $5–$10 a day. Step Digital says there is no single right daily budget. Those are their methods.
- Daily budget can run about 25% over on a busy day and still balance across the week. That is Meta’s budget documentation, not a glitch.
- Do not copy a ROAS from a Twitter thread. You do not have their margin.
:::

## The only Meta number that is about learning

[About the learning phase](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/112167992830700) says the delivery system is still exploring after a new ad set or a significant edit. The Delivery column shows Learning. Ad sets usually exit once they can deliver in a stable way, “usually after about 50 results in the week after the ad set’s last significant edit.” A result is whatever you optimised for. Fifty purchases is a different week from fifty adds to cart.

[Meta’s note on website campaigns](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/910877842876429) repeats the same bar: aim for at least about 50 optimisation events a week, and consider a higher-frequency event if purchases are too rare. It also says to consolidate ad sets so the budget is not split into pieces that each stay in learning.

If your target cost per purchase is $40, fifty purchases in a week is $2,000 of spend before you have “exited learning” on a purchase objective. Most small Shopify brands will not do that in month one. [Step Digital’s guide](https://stepdigital.co/guides/how-much-to-spend-on-meta-ads-per-day) makes that arithmetic explicit and says a small store should judge earlier signals, such as click-through and adds to cart, instead of pretending the 50-purchase week is mandatory. We agree with the shape of that advice. The Australian dollar examples on their page are theirs. The Meta citation under them is the part you can check.

## What the budget pages actually say

[Meta’s budget documentation](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/marketing-api/bidding/overview/budgets/) describes a daily budget as the average you are willing to spend. On some days the system may spend up to 25% more. Their example is a $10 budget that can become $12.50 on a high-opportunity day. Over the week it is meant to settle. If you refresh Ads Manager at noon and panic, you will edit the campaign and restart learning. Do not do that because of the 25% line.

The same page says amounts are in the minor unit of the ad account currency, such as cents, and that Ads Manager enforces the minimum that applies to your bid type. There is no sentence in that document that says “every Shopify store must spend $50 a day”.

[Shopify’s Facebook ads cost article](https://www.shopify.com/blog/facebook-ads-cost) (their 2025 update) says the minimum advertising budget for a Facebook ad is $1 per day for campaigns billed by impressions, and that a minimum of $5 per day is recommended for more aggressive campaigns such as traffic or sales. Treat the $1 as Shopify’s statement of the impression floor, and confirm the field in your own Ads Manager. Treat the $5 as Shopify’s recommendation, not as a law of profit.

[Talk Shop’s 2026 Shopify budget matrix](https://www.letstalkshop.com/blog/how-much-to-spend-on-facebook-ads-for-new-shopify-store) is a different document. Pre-validation, with no sales and no pixel history, they suggest $5–$10 a day. Validation, once there are a few sales, $10–$30. Their later rows go much higher and assume a profitable campaign. That is a playbook from a studio that wants you to test tightly. It is not Meta.

## A way to choose without borrowing someone else’s ROAS

Start from the margin, the way Step Digital argues, not from a viral daily budget.

1. Take an average order.
2. Remove product cost, packaging, shipping and payment fees. What is left is the most the order can spend on ads before that order loses money. Your accountant’s version will be cleaner than this sketch. Use theirs if you have it.
3. Decide how many ads you will test. One campaign, one ad set, two or three ads is enough while the budget is small. Splitting $10 across four ad sets starves all of them.
4. Run it for 14 days unless the product page is obviously broken. Talk Shop’s early window is in that neighbourhood. You can stop sooner if the shop is paying for clicks to a 404.
5. Judge the result you chose. If you optimised for landing page views, do not declare the ads a failure because purchases are still a handful.

If fifty purchases a week is fantasy, optimise for a more frequent event only if that event still means something. An add to cart is weaker than a purchase and stronger than a random click. Say which one you are buying.

Raise the budget in small steps after the result holds, not the same afternoon you change the hero image. A significant edit sends the ad set back into learning. Meta’s help page is blunt about that. A new photo and a doubled budget on Monday is two edits. You will not know which one changed Thursday.

## A fourteen-day card you can fill in

Write this before you launch.

- Daily budget, and the 25% overage so the week’s ceiling does not surprise you.
- One objective.
- The event that counts as a result.
- How many results you would need in a week to be near Meta’s “about 50”.
- The order margin, so you know when a purchase campaign is structurally too poor to learn.
- The date you will read the account, and the one change you are allowed to make that day.

Then leave it alone. The blogs you bookmarked can wait until that date. If you want a second pair of eyes on the export, [send the brief](/contact). Bring the margin and the result name. Leave the borrowed ROAS in the other tab.

## Creative and the catalogue, because budget cannot rescue them

A small daily budget has no room for five products and three offers. Pick the product you can fulfil this month, with photos that match the variant the customer will receive. If the ad shows a blue mug and the page opens on a red one, you will pay for the confusion. Shopify’s product feed and Meta’s catalogue either agree or they do not. Check one product by hand before you trust the sync.

Write the first line as a person. Name the product and the reason to buy it now, if there is a real reason. A fake countdown is a good way to train buyers to ignore you. Shipping time belongs in the ad if it is slow. Surprise shipping time belongs in your refund pile.

Two or three ads in one ad set are enough: the same product, a different first line or a different photo. Meta can favour one. You can turn the loser off after the reading date, not after six hours. That is the whole creative test a small budget can support. More variants than that, and you are back to splitting a thin spend.

If the shop is new, say so in the brief to yourself. Talk Shop’s pre-validation row exists because a new domain and an empty pixel are a different problem from a shop with a year of orders. You can still advertise. You should not expect the learning phase to close on purchases. Expect a bill for information: which photo people click, whether anyone adds to cart, whether the page loads on a phone. That information is the point of the first fourteen days. Scaling is a later conversation, and the [five checks](/blog/facebook-ads-roi-tips) are the gate.
